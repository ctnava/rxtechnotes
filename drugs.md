# Common Drugs & Uses

## How Drugs Are Classified

USAN uses **stems and class designations** to group drugs with similar:

- Mechanisms of action  
- Therapeutic uses  
- Chemical structures  

### Key Points About Drug Classification

- Drug class assignment is proposed during the USAN naming process.  
- USAN and the sponsor agree on a **pharmacologic or therapeutic classification**, but this classification is **not legally official**.  
- Different references may classify the same drug differently.  
- Stems and syllables evolve over time as new drug classes emerge.

### 🔹 **CLASSES**

`A *class* is a **category** a drug belongs to, independent of its name.`

Classes are **groupings of drugs** based on shared **therapeutic use**, **mechanism of action**, or **chemical characteristics**.

They organize drugs for clinical reference, education, and prescribing.

**Examples**:

- ACE inhibitors  
- Beta‑blockers  
- Antifungals  
- SSRIs  

### 🔹 **STEMS**

`A *stem* is a **naming tool** built into the generic name itself to tie a drug to a class.`

Stems are **specific syllables** placed at the beginning, middle, or end of a **generic drug name** to signal something about the drug’s **mechanism, structure, or family**.

They help clinicians instantly recognize what type of drug they’re dealing with.

**Examples**:

- **‑pril** → ACE inhibitors  
- **‑olol** → Beta‑blockers  
- **‑mab** → Monoclonal antibodies  
- **‑azole** → Antifungals

## Classification Schemes

`There is no single standardized classification scheme used in medicine.`

### Classification by Nervous System Response

A number of classifications are based on whether drugs influence the parasympathetic or sympathetic nervous system.  
Most organs in the body are influenced by both systems. These systems generally stimulate opposing responses, which balance their effects and maintain a normal state of homeostasis.

**Homeostasis**  
A stable internal environment maintained by balancing physiological processes.

Drugs that act on the parasympathetic system are called **cholinergic**, because acetylcholine is the neurotransmitter of this system.

**Neurotransmitter**  
A chemical messenger released by neurons to transmit signals across synapses to other neurons, muscles, or glands.

Drugs that act on the sympathetic nervous system are called **adrenergic**, because the neurotransmitters norepinephrine and epinephrine are secreted from the adrenal glands.

### Classification by Receptor Activity

Drugs may be classified by how they interact with specific receptors.

- **Agonists**: activate a receptor  
- **Antagonists**: block a receptor  
- **Partial agonists**: activate a receptor but produce a weaker response  

Adrenergic receptor responses may be categorized as **alpha** and **beta** receptors.  
Each receptor subtype is responsible for specific pharmacological effects.

### Additional Factors That Complicate Naming Schemes

Certain naming conventions overlap or cause confusion:

- **Blocker**: usually means antagonist  
- **Anti‑**: often means antagonist (e.g., antihistamine)  
- **‑mimetic**: means agonist

Other neurotransmitter systems also influence classification, including:

- Serotonin  
- Dopamine  
- Histamine  
- Gamma‑aminobutyric acid (GABA)

Each neurotransmitter has multiple receptor subtypes, each with agonists and antagonists that act through various mechanisms.
